Select a stage to see its decisions and checks. Accurate locations and order status keep the physical work and inventory record aligned.

MATERIAL FLOW

Why goods spend time in storage

Warehouses buffer changes in supply and demand, combine loads and delay some decisions until more is known. These functions explain planned waiting; unnecessary delays still need investigation.

INFORMATION FLOW

Keep the inventory record current

A warehouse management system (WMS) coordinates inventory and work; a warehouse control system (WCS) directs equipment. Item identity, location, quantity and status need to agree across their interfaces. A dashboard is only as useful as the underlying records.

Look at the handoff. A receiving error can lead to a failed pick later. The explorer’s checks are prompts for review, not measured class results.

Choosing a system · full notes

With the process in view, compare equipment by the work it must handle and the handoffs it creates.

Start with the order profile

Frequently requested products need easy access and replenishment. A broad range of slow movers needs space and reliable retrieval. Measure the actual demand pattern rather than assuming an “80/20” split.

Design racks and robots together

One reflection compares rack-climbing robots with separate machines for vertical and horizontal movement. Separate machines create a transfer that must be coordinated. It also asks whether products ordered together should be stored together, alongside decisions based on popularity.

Moving a container is only part of picking. A tote or shelf brought to a workstation still needs its individual items selected and checked. The separate essay raises this distinction through Brightpick, whose manufacturer describes robotic item picking with goods-to-person fallback. The description explains the mechanism; performance needs its own comparison.

Choose how the work is organized

Bucket brigades, mixed single/batch picking and flow picking organize work in different ways. They are operating methods rather than robot types. One reflection notes that fixed zones can develop uneven workloads.

Student views on automation · full notes

The process and equipment comparisons raise a broader question: what improves for workers and smaller firms, and what new dependencies appear?

OPPORTUNITY

Less heavy work and more accessible tools

  • Some students welcome robots for heavy or repetitive handling, leaving people to manage exceptions.
  • Specialized equipment and small modular pilots could make adoption more manageable for smaller warehouses.
  • Better layouts, records and work methods can help manual or hybrid operations before major equipment purchases.

QUESTIONS TO KEEP OPEN

Skills, fairness and resilience

  • One reflection worries that goods-to-person work may reduce skills, autonomy and involvement.
  • It also asks whether removing spare capacity and alternative methods makes outages harder to handle.
  • Worker monitoring raises questions about fairness and privacy. Training, trust and participation need attention during adoption.
01 / PRODUCT FIT

Check product requirements

Healthcare and fresh-food examples add temperature, shelf life, packaging and careful handling to the comparison.

02 / SMALL-FIRM ACCESS

Include the cost of changing systems

Beyond purchase price, include maintenance, integration, downtime, training, energy and replacement. Shared or service-based automation is an option to examine; affordability still needs testing.

03 / PEAKS + RECOVERY

Prepare for peaks and outages

One proposal pairs a normal automated workload with manual support at peaks. Test surge demand, equipment failures and exception recovery alongside normal throughput.

These perspectives differ. Evaluating automation means considering service, worker experience and resilience together.

Evaluate the evidence · full notes

Use the earlier examples and student questions to decide what an improvement should be measured against.

MEASURE THE OPERATION

Use the same units and process boundary

  • Little’s Law: average inventory or work in process L = λW. For a stable, consistently defined flow, 120 orders/hour × 0.5 hour = 60 orders in process on average.
  • Time in process includes waiting. The relation alone does not show that reducing stock causes faster service.
  • Define throughput, turnaround, pick accuracy, queues, energy and downtime for the same process and operating conditions.
  • High storage density can coexist with access delays, digging, station queues or slow replenishment.

READ CLAIMS CAREFULLY

Read examples in their setting

  • Four app-timing observations are a convenience sample, not a representative fulfillment benchmark.
  • Dedicated/shared storage percentages in reflections are model examples, not universal utilization targets.
  • Reported vendor savings and country comparisons need independent evidence before they can support a broader conclusion.
  • The lecture’s improvement figures are illustrations, not class measurements. The reservation paper tests a particular method and dataset; its measure and percentage differ from the slide summary.
