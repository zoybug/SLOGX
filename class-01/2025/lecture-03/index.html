Intelligent warehousing

Intelligent warehousing system

Lecture 03 · 29 September 2025 · Lecturer: Prof. Peng Yang

Lecture overview

A warehouse buffers supply and demand, consolidates shipments and prepares customer orders. Peng Yang follows the work from receiving and put-away to picking, checking, packing and shipping, then compares tote-handling, grid-storage, rack-climbing and shelf-moving systems. The lecture treats equipment and operating rules as complementary choices: storage assignment, order batching, task sequencing and energy-aware scheduling determine how the hardware performs. Its final discussion considers human–robot collaboration, sustainability and the need to evaluate the complete order-fulfilment process.

Lecturer profile

Peng Yang is an associate professor at Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School. His research covers robotic warehousing, AI in warehousing and logistics, dynamic order picking, and sustainable logistics. These interests connect the lecture’s equipment comparisons with decisions about how warehouses are operated.

Student synthesis

This section brings together anonymized coursework from the Fall 2025 class. We reviewed the submitted responses, compared the ideas raised, and grouped recurring themes while preserving meaningful differences in perspective. Student names and IDs are not shown. The findings describe the submitted work, rather than the views of every student or a measured outcome of the lecture.

Assignment — What did students learn about intelligent warehousing?

Students wrote a two-page reflection on the lecture. The assignment was open-ended; the themes below organize their responses rather than represent separate homework questions. The preparation task asked them to compare robotic warehousing solutions and consider what efficient operation requires.

Why storage and inventory records must agree

What students said

  • Students increasingly described a warehouse as a sequence of connected operations, rather than a building for storing stock. Receiving, put-away, picking and dispatch each depend on what the preceding stage records.
  • Several responses focused on item identity, location and status. They linked barcodes, RFID and warehouse software to practical decisions such as finding stock, releasing work and correcting exceptions.
  • The distinction between a warehouse management system, which coordinates inventory and work, and a warehouse control system, which directs equipment, helped some students understand how software and physical handling fit together.

What this suggests: These reflections suggest that reliable information is an operating requirement: faster handling helps only when the next task can trust the record it receives.

What determines the right picking system?

What students said

  • The equipment demonstrations drew attention to different ways of reaching goods: retrieving stacked bins, climbing racks, moving totes or bringing shelves to workstations. Students did not identify a single system as best for every warehouse.
  • Responses connected system choice with the product and order profile. Frequently requested items need convenient access; a broad range of slow movers raises different space and retrieval questions. Healthcare and fresh-food examples added temperature, shelf-life and handling requirements.
  • Several students examined the handoffs created by the design. Separate vertical and horizontal robots must coordinate a transfer, while delivering a container to a workstation still leaves individual items to be selected and checked.

What this suggests: The useful comparison is between complete ways of fulfilling the same work, including access, replenishment and workstation capacity.

How operating rules change fulfilment

What students said

  • Students distinguished new equipment from better operating methods. They discussed batching, bucket brigades, flow picking and storage assignment as ways to organize work in manual, hybrid or robotic warehouses.
  • One response used an uneven picking-zone workload to explain why a quick local operation can leave an entire order waiting. Others connected product placement and order reservation with less repeated travel.
  • Reflections that used Little’s Law linked average inventory or work in process, throughput and time in process. Their examples prompted a broader question: does a change improve full order turnaround, or only one stage?

What this suggests: A shorter route and a better service outcome are different results. Travel, waiting, deadlines and downstream queues need to be considered together.

What students expected automation to change

What students said

  • Some students welcomed robots for reducing walking, lifting and repetitive handling, and imagined people taking on maintenance, supervision and exception work.
  • Other responses questioned whether fixed goods-to-person tasks could reduce skills and autonomy, or whether close coupling and little spare capacity would make outages harder to handle. Worker monitoring also raised fairness and privacy concerns.
  • Smaller-firm access was a recurring question. Students proposed modular pilots, shared automation or service-based access, while recognizing that integration, maintenance, downtime and training add to the purchase price.

What this suggests: The reflections support evaluating worker experience, affordability and recovery from failure alongside normal-day throughput; they express different expectations rather than a class-wide verdict.

Quiz

Twelve practice questions apply the lecture’s process, system-choice and measurement concepts; they do not affect course grades.

Inventory records and physical handoffs

Original assignment wording (verbatim): Write a 2-page report to reflect on what you have learnt from the lecture.

The original task asked for a reflection on what was learned, without prescribing a list of topics. Responses that explained receiving and put-away commonly emphasized scanning both the incoming item and its actual storage location. Other responses used WMS/WCS analogies to make the interfaces easier to understand; those analogies are student explanations, not formal system specifications.

One reflection traced how an error at receiving could reappear when an order was picked or packed. Another connected incomplete stock information with practical difficulties in a partly automated healthcare warehouse. These examples support attention to interfaces and error recovery; they do not establish a measured error reduction.

The supplied lecture deck lists receiving, put-away, order-picking, checking/packing and shipping. The operating-stage explorer is an editorial teaching interpretation of that sequence. Its final Review stage adds a whole-process check; it is not a sixth operation listed in the lecture or an extra assignment question.

Buffering supply and demand and consolidating shipments are functions of a warehouse. Planned storage time should be distinguished from avoidable queues or a missing-stock investigation.

Equipment fit, access and item picking

The preparation task invited students to examine solution providers and think about each solution’s advantages, disadvantages and operating issues. The assessed reflection remained open-ended. The system comparisons here combine the supplied lecture demonstrations with the questions students raised afterwards.

One reflection contrasted a rack-climbing robot with a design that separates vertical retrieval from floor transport. Another asked whether products frequently ordered together should be stored near one another. Both point to the interaction between layout and operating rules, rather than the speed of an isolated machine.

For stacked-bin systems, retrieving a buried bin can require moving bins above it. Product popularity and reshuffling therefore matter alongside storage density. The lecture’s AutoStore example and the manufacturer’s mechanism explanation support this distinction, but do not demonstrate a universal space or throughput gain.

Moving a tote or shelf is different from grasping its individual products. Brightpick’s manufacturer explanation describes robotic item picking with goods-to-person fallback; it is a mechanism description, not independent comparative performance evidence.

Some responses discuss a broader warehousing course or add outside technologies. The overview attributes only topics present in this lecture deck to Peng Yang’s supplied session. Numerical SKU ratios, storage-utilization examples and product specifications in individual responses are not adopted as universal design rules.

Operating methods and performance measures

The deck explicitly separates innovative systems from smart methods. It names dynamic zoning/bucket brigades, mixed single-order and batch picking, flow picking and predictive order reservation. These are ways of organizing work, not interchangeable robot types.

A response about fixed zones describes an order waiting for its slowest zone. Another values bucket brigades because the work can be shared more evenly. These are explanations and student interpretations; the class did not measure a before-and-after productivity change.

Little’s Law is L = λW, with compatible units and the same process boundary. In a stable illustrative flow, 120 orders/hour multiplied by 0.5 hour gives 60 orders in process on average. Time in process includes waiting. The identity does not show that cutting inventory by itself causes faster service, or that throughput can rise without addressing the relevant constraints.

One response reports four convenience observations from order-status screens used in class. Those observations concern the recorded order/picking timestamps; they are not a representative warehouse benchmark or total delivery-time measurement. Neither country comparisons nor supplier productivity claims in the reflections establish general performance.

The lecture cites Kang, Qu and Yang’s predictive-reservation research. The webpage’s three-order exercise is a separate constructed example. Its known arrivals, straight aisle and omitted handling work make it useful for explaining a trade-off, but not for reproducing that study’s algorithm or results.

A fair pilot would define throughput, order turnaround, pick accuracy, queues, energy and downtime for comparable products, demand and process boundaries. Different workload patterns and forecast errors can change which release rule is useful.

Work, affordability and recovery

One response sees human–robot collaboration as a way to remove physically demanding work and create technical roles. A contrasting response worries about deskilling and alienation at fixed workstations. The synthesis preserves both perspectives without implying that either outcome was observed across the class.

Training and usable interfaces mattered in responses about partly automated warehouses. Another reflection warned against misuse of individual performance data. These concerns call for worker involvement and fair arrangements for monitoring, not merely a throughput target.

Students interested in smaller warehouses proposed affordable modular systems and shared or service-based automation. These remain proposals: the submitted work does not establish that such arrangements are financially viable for a particular firm.

A suggested hybrid arrangement uses automation for routine demand and manual support at peaks. Another response questions whether removing redundancy weakens resilience. Both make recovery, alternative methods and dependence on critical handoffs relevant to evaluation.

The lecture also includes energy-aware scheduling and sustainability. Its improvement figures refer to particular illustrative or research settings; this page does not adopt them as savings measured by students.

Batching assumptions and calculations

There is one straight aisle with the depot at 0 m and shelves at 40 m and 80 m. Order 1 arrives at minute 0 for the 40 m shelf and is due at minute 3, or minute 2 when urgent. Order 2 arrives at minute 1 for the 80 m shelf and is due at minute 4. Order 3 arrives at minute 3 for the 40 m shelf and is due at minute 4.

The picker travels at 80 m/min, returns to the depot after each tour, and completes every order in a batch together on return. Completion is departure time plus tour distance divided by 80 m/min. Mean turnaround averages completion minus arrival for the three orders. An order is late only if completion is after its due time.

Immediate release uses tours of 80, 160 and 80 m, completing at minutes 1, 3 and 4: 320 m total travel, 1.33 min mean turnaround and no late orders. Bounded reservation groups orders 1 and 2 at minute 1, completing them at minute 3; order 3 finishes at minute 4: 240 m, 2.00 min, and no late orders unless order 1 is urgent, when one is late. Waiting for all orders releases them at minute 3 and completes all at minute 5: 160 m, 3.67 min and three late orders.

The arrivals are known in advance here. Actual predictive reservation must consider forecast error, due times and resource availability. This example does not reproduce the research algorithm, and its outputs are calculated teaching values rather than warehouse or student measurements.
