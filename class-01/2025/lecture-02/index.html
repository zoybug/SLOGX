Smart Transportation and Distribution

Smart Transportation and Distribution

Lecture 02 · 22 September 2025 · Lecturer: Prof. Mingyao Qi

Lecture overview

A transportation service must assign work, sequence stops and coordinate vehicles and transfers within time, capacity and energy limits. Prof. Mingyao Qi begins with freight modes, intermodal handoffs and short-haul services, including retail replenishment, instant delivery and medical logistics. The lecture then examines electric vehicles and charging, modular vehicles, port AGVs, urban air mobility, truck–drone cooperation and mobile parcel lockers. Routing, scheduling, location and packing methods connect these technologies to feasible operating plans. The central comparison is between improving one leg of a journey and improving the service as a whole.

Lecturer profile

Mingyao Qi is a professor at Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School. His research covers modeling, optimization and simulation of logistics systems, including service network design, vehicle routing and logistics robotics.

Student synthesis

This section brings together anonymized coursework from the Fall 2025 class. We reviewed the submitted responses, compared the ideas raised, and grouped recurring themes while preserving meaningful differences in perspective. Student names and IDs are not shown. The findings describe the submitted work, rather than the views of every student or a measured outcome of the lecture.

Assignment — Introduce a smart transportation application

Write a 2-page report to introduce a smart transportation application.

Students were asked to introduce one smart transportation application. The themes below compare the service problems, decisions and limits in those reports; they are synthesis groupings, not separate homework questions.

Delivery range, energy and vehicle coordination

  • Drone reports linked urgent or hard-to-reach deliveries with limits on payload, battery endurance and weather. Truck–drone proposals made the meeting point and timing part of the service, rather than treating the flight separately.
  • Electric-fleet reports compared charging, swapping and infrastructure choices. They showed why a route needs a reachable, available source of energy at a time compatible with its delivery commitments.
  • One report specifically questioned fixed drone range by considering energy consumption that changes with payload. Another raised the risk that charging technology could change during a road investment’s long service life.

What this suggests. These reports make feasibility more specific: the usable route depends on load, energy, infrastructure and the timing of the next handoff.

Passenger access and transfers

  • The passenger reports included urban air mobility and a smart-bus application. They considered vehicle technology together with stops or vertiports, timetables, connections and traveler information.
  • UAM reports raised certification, weather, charging infrastructure, noise and public acceptance. These issues differ from the stop and passenger-service commitments in a bus system.
  • Some reports emphasized the prospect of faster aerial travel; others gave more attention to affordability, infrastructure, supervision and integration with ground transport.

What this suggests. A passenger service should be considered from origin to destination, including access and transfer time. That is an editorial evaluation question arising from the reports, not a travel-time result measured by the class.

Data services and container handoffs

  • Navigation, traffic-management, telematics and shipment-visibility reports described different uses of data: choosing a path, predicting an arrival, managing maintenance or responding to an exception.
  • A navigation report drew attention to map maintenance, cloud infrastructure and security behind the visible interface. A telematics report also raised training, legacy-system integration and privacy.
  • The port report connected horizontal AGV movement with crane handling. Faster vehicles can still wait if the next resource is unavailable.
  • One visibility-platform report explicitly stated that the forum presentation lacked a clear demonstration or commercialization. That uncertainty belongs alongside the description of its proposed functions.

What this suggests. Better observations help only when they reach the right decision and a workable handoff. The comparison must distinguish an available feature, a proposed capability and a demonstrated service outcome.

Mixed services and proposed systems

  • Broader reports connected several modes and facilities, rather than one device. The CityFlow Connect proposal combines van micro-hubs, cargo bikes, couriers and mobile lockers with shared routing and customer-location data.
  • Another reflection proposed drones reaching difficult destinations and handing parcels to a ground service. A companion reflection linked data, hardware and algorithms as parts of one service design.
  • The proposals make transfers, shared commitments and data permissions visible. They also expose questions about investment, trust and who handles exceptions when several operators are involved.
  • An individual reflection asked for shorter research segments and more discussion time. This is a learning preference supplied with a Lecture 02 reflection, rather than a second multi-question assignment for the class.

What this suggests. The integration ideas are useful because they state what must work together. They remain proposals and interpretations until their operating assumptions and outcomes are tested.

Teaching interpretation

From operating data to a coordinated service

A navigation feed, a battery estimate and a crane-status record each inform a different decision. This six-stage explanation follows how that information can support a coordinated service; it was constructed for the archive from lecture concepts and student examples.

Select a stage to follow its information, decision and handoff requirements.

Connection

This is a conceptual interpretation, not a model that predicts savings or a framework attributed to the lecturer.

Compare the operating decisions

The application reports concern different services. This comparison uses the same four questions—information, decision, constraints and outcomes to examine—to show how their operating requirements differ. The outcome measures are teaching suggestions, not results measured by the students.

This comparison describes operating requirements. It calculates no savings or validated service result.

What must be checked before claiming a service improvement?

The reports mix descriptions of current applications, research methods and new concepts. A useful comparison first identifies the service, the decision being changed and the baseline against which the benefit will be judged.

  • Check that the method solves the required problem: a shortest path is one leg, whereas a fleet plan also assigns work, sequences stops and satisfies operating constraints.
  • Compare equivalent service, with access, waiting, failed deliveries and relevant energy or infrastructure included. A result in one research model or demonstration does not establish a general deployment gain.
  • Keep the status of the evidence visible: proposed system, controlled demonstration, operator description and independently measured result have different meanings.

These are editorial evaluation questions, not a new assignment or student-measured outcomes.

Delivery range, energy and vehicle coordination

The student descriptions concern different missions: parcel delivery, surveillance and passenger transport are not interchangeable. The Nested-VRP lecture reference models a surveillance drone that periodically meets a truck to exchange batteries.

The payload-dependent energy report discusses a thesis and alternative algorithms. Its claimed numerical improvement is not adopted here as an independently verified result.

Wireless charging transfers energy supplied by infrastructure. It does not create a self-recovering energy source; vehicle range and net environmental gains still need operating evidence.

Passenger access and transfers

Six primary-focus records concern UAM and one concerns smart buses. This describes the submitted topics, not relative student support for those services.

A demonstration flight shows a capability in the reported setup. It does not establish routine passenger operations, service affordability or a general door-to-door time saving.

The lecture uses UAM alongside advanced air mobility. The FAA companion framework treats urban air mobility as part of the broader AAM field; this clarification does not rename the original student topics.

Data services and container handoffs

The named applications include Baidu Maps, Itsumo NAVI, intelligent transportation systems and telematics/UPS ORION. Their algorithms and benefits as described by students are not independently confirmed here unless a primary reference supports the specific claim.

The UPS operator source separates ORION’s stop ordering from UPSNav’s turn-by-turn directions. An aggregate saving in a student source was mislabeled as a per-route saving and is not repeated as fact.

The port report motivates a joint crane–vehicle scheduling question. The suggested waiting, turnaround and throughput measures in the teaching comparison are evaluation ideas, not measurements collected by the class.

The forum-described visibility platform remains a student description with unresolved deployment evidence. No source is invented to make it appear verified.

Mixed services and proposed systems

CityFlow Connect is an original student design proposal in the submitted work, not an established commercial service. Its van–bike–locker handoffs and access rules would need validation.

The data–hardware–algorithms formulation is the student’s interpretation, not a named framework quoted from the lecturer.

The qualitative synthesis retains two distinct Lecture 02 reflections found with other lecture batches. One complements a platform report by the same student; it does not add a second independent person to the topic chart.

Application topics and counting method

Original assignment wording

This was one open application task, not Q1–Q5 or six assigned categories. The six chart families are editorial groupings of the reports’ primary subjects.

The chart counts 30 students once each. For a student who supplied both an application report and a distinct reflection, the application report determines the primary category and both texts inform the qualitative discussion.

Passenger mobility includes six UAM focuses and one smart-bus focus. Integrated surveys/concepts includes six broad surveys or reflections and the CityFlow proposal. An unattributed exact repetition of the CityFlow text is excluded.

Counts describe the supplied work; course enrollment is not inferred.

From operating data to a coordinated service

The stages are observe, model, choose, coordinate, execute and review. They need not occur as a single fixed sequence; an exception may require an earlier assumption to be reconsidered.

A prediction or digital twin represents a condition. A route, charging decision or dispatch instruction chooses an action, and the service depends on whether partners can execute it together.

Review feasibility under capacity, timing, energy and permissions; coordinate vehicles, cranes, couriers and customers; and compare total service and resource use with a baseline.

Application comparison: scope and assumptions

A shorter route or faster vehicle can still leave a queue, missed transfer or unmet commitment elsewhere in the service.

Drone delivery and drone–truck surveillance are distinct missions. Electric fleets couple energy decisions with time windows; UAM and buses impose different passenger-access requirements.

The linked studies, regulator framework and operator descriptions provide separate evidence. They do not validate every student-described application or the proposed CityFlow system.

Routing methods and service evidence

The lecture’s example that exact methods solve about 100 customers is an illustrative scale, not a universal ceiling. Runtime depends on the instance, formulation, algorithm and computing budget. Optimality for a model requires a valid proof or bound.

For the lecture’s symmetric Clarke–Wright example, the saving is c(0,i) + c(0,j) − c(i,j). Try larger savings first and merge only compatible route endpoints that satisfy capacity and time constraints.

Nearest insertion selects an unvisited stop close to the current tour and then considers its least-cost feasible insertion position. It is not a guarantee of superiority over nearest neighbor on every instance. Metaheuristic search may temporarily explore worse or infeasible states; the dispatched plan still must be feasible.

Electric propulsion removes tailpipe exhaust in use. Net environmental impact depends on the comparison and boundary, including relevant electricity, batteries and infrastructure. Wireless charging transfers supplied energy; it does not generate free energy.

The airborne warehouse is a patent or concept, the CityFlow system is a student proposal, and a flight demonstration is evidence of capability under its reported conditions. None is silently relabeled as routine commercial operation.

Construction and improvement methods

Construction heuristics build an initial route. Local-search methods such as 2-opt and 3-opt change route edges, while metaheuristics explore a wider set of alternatives. A learned dispatch policy still requires capacity, timing and other feasibility checks.

Vehicle-specific limits

Payload and endurance depend on the vehicle. Passenger aircraft and parcel drones have different requirements. Neither the reports nor the teaching comparison establish universal cost shares, free battery swapping or general safety gains.
