High-precision positioning

High Accuracy Positioning for ITS

Lecture 05 · 20 October 2025 · Lecturer: Prof. Kai Zhang

Lecture overview

Navigation, fleet tracking, emergency response, bus-arrival information and road pricing depend on knowing where a vehicle is. Kai Zhang’s lecture explains why that position remains uncertain: satellite signals can be blocked or reflected, while inertial estimates accumulate error. It follows three connected tasks: combining global navigation satellite system (GNSS), inertial and vehicle measurements; updating a position estimate and its uncertainty; and associating noisy positions with roads. A hidden Markov model (HMM) and Viterbi inference compare candidate road sequences, while Fréchet methods compare curve geometry. The service determines how accurate, available and timely the information must be.

Lecturer profile

Kai Zhang is an associate professor at Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School. His official profile lists teaching in multisensor fusion and intelligent transportation, and research in information fusion, statistical signal processing and high-accuracy position and attitude estimation.

Student synthesis

This section brings together anonymized coursework from the Fall 2025 class. We reviewed the submitted responses, compared the ideas raised, and grouped recurring themes while preserving meaningful differences in perspective. Student names and IDs are not shown. The findings describe the submitted work, rather than the views of every student or a measured outcome of the lecture.

What interested students most about the positioning lecture?

The assignment asked for a one-page report on the part of the lecture that interested each student most. It was an open reflection, with no required list of separate questions.

Why satellite and inertial measurements are combined

Students were drawn to the complementary weaknesses of satellite positioning and an inertial navigation system (INS): reflected or missing satellite signals, and inertial drift between external updates. Some began with the assumption that GPS alone was sufficient; others focused on how a filter carries uncertainty through prediction and correction.

How a sequence of fixes identifies the traveled road

Map matching attracted both probabilistic and geometric explanations. Students discussed hidden road candidates, observation likelihoods and plausible transitions in an HMM. Others found the dog-and-owner analogy for Fréchet distance easier to visualize, while recognizing that similar road shapes can remain ambiguous.

Different levels of mathematical confidence

Some reports pursued state-estimation equations, sensor errors and free-space diagrams. Others valued the analogies but found the lecture dense and wanted a clearer understanding of the Kalman filter. Interest in a method therefore did not always mean confidence in applying it.

Choosing a system for the service and its budget

Students connected positioning with fleet management, road pricing and everyday navigation. They also raised sensor cost, calibration, integration complexity and map quality, and explored RTK, UWB or additional sensors as possible extensions. These were interests and proposals, rather than demonstrated improvements in cost or safety.

The reflections suggest that the useful lesson is not a single accuracy claim: readers need to understand how uncertain measurements, a road model and a service requirement fit together. The teaching examples below isolate those relationships.

Quiz

Twelve practice questions on sensor errors, uncertainty, road inference and application requirements; they do not affect the course grade.

Original lecture assignment

Write a 1-page report on what interested you the most in the lecture.

The theme headings organize the submitted work; they are not additional assignment questions.

Why satellite and inertial measurements are combined

One reflection describes revising an earlier belief that GPS was already accurate enough for city navigation. Others use the cooperation between sensors to make the mathematics intuitive, or work through covariance equations. These are different ways of understanding the same lecture concept. A filter does not automatically learn every bias or guarantee accuracy during an outage: calibration, the motion model and the assumed error statistics still matter.

How a sequence of fixes identifies the traveled road

The reflections distinguish knowing a coordinate from identifying the road being traveled. One connects HMM inference with previous mapping work; another follows up the Fréchet example with independent study of dynamic programming. The standard leash analogy preserves forward traversal order while allowing different speeds. It does not require identical timestamps or reduce to a shortest-path routing problem. The lecture’s online/offline distinction concerns when a result is produced; incremental/global concerns how much of a trace is considered.

Choosing a system for the service and its budget

Reports discussing road pricing and fleet services emphasize why a wrong location can affect a decision. Others ask whether a more expensive sensor or tighter integration is justified for the intended application. Loose and tight GNSS/INS coupling describe whether GNSS solutions or lower-level observables enter the estimator; feedback/feed-forward is a separate correction-design choice. A satellite communications link carries information, whereas GNSS observations contribute to estimating location. The two should not be treated as interchangeable evidence of positioning reliability.

Mathematical understanding and curve matching

A mathematically detailed reflection works through prediction and covariance updates; a different student explicitly says the Kalman filter was not yet understood in class and plans to return to it later. Another asks how unresolved map-matching challenges can be handled in practice. These differences support introducing an intuitive example before returning to the full equations, rather than presenting the class as uniformly convinced or technically fluent.

For continuous Fréchet distance, both traversals move forward while their speeds may differ. A free-space diagram marks curve-point pairs within a chosen distance threshold; a forward path through that space tests whether the threshold permits a complete matching. Similar road geometries can still produce ambiguous matches. The discrete variant in Resources compares finite vertex sequences and should be distinguished from the continuous definition.

How measurements become usable position information

GNSS contributes an external position reference. Accelerometers and gyroscopes measure motion; odometer and vehicle signals can add constraints. A motion model propagates state and uncertainty, and available external observations can correct that prediction. Road geometry and connectivity then help identify the traveled link. Map matching estimates a past or current road; route planning chooses a future journey. Timestamps, units, reference frames, calibration and map quality must agree throughout.

How much should a new GNSS fix change a prediction?

The measurement observes the same one-dimensional position directly, so H = 1. P is the predicted position-error variance and R is the measurement-error variance, both in m²; they are the squares of the two standard-deviation controls. With independent, unbiased errors and valid variances, K = P/(P + R), x⁺ = 8 + K(z − 8), and P⁺ = (1 − K)P. At the defaults, P = 4 m², R = 9 m², K = 4/13 ≈ 0.308, x⁺ ≈ 9.23 m, and σ⁺ ≈ 1.66 m.

Larger R reduces the influence of the fix; larger P increases it. If the fix is unavailable, x and P are unchanged by this measurement step. Subsequent predictions may increase uncertainty through process noise. The smaller reported variance is a result under these assumptions, not an independent measurement of actual accuracy or a detector of multipath bias.

Can three noisy fixes support a plausible road sequence?

The presets use y observations [1, 6, 1] m and [1, 9, 9] m. Each sample has two road candidates with equal initial probabilities 0.5. A candidate’s log observation likelihood is −0.5((y − road_y)/σ)²; the Gaussian normalization term is common to all candidates and can be omitted when selecting the best path. Without connectors the transition probabilities are 1 for staying and 0 for changing road. With connectors they are 0.9 and 0.1. Viterbi adds log transition and observation likelihoods, keeps the best predecessor at each step, then backtracks to recover the highest-scoring sequence.

At σ = 4 m, [1, 6, 1] gives nearest A → B → A but Viterbi A → A → A. For [1, 9, 9], disconnected roads give B → B → B; allowing connectors gives A → B → B. The geometry is schematic: connectors only permit a change between samples. Heading, speed, travel time, route distance, lane identity and elevation are absent. These transition weights are illustrative choices, not fitted driving probabilities or the full Newson–Krumm method.

Candidate selection in the lecture

The lecture also describes pruning candidate roads using distance and plausible route-distance or speed limits. These checks reduce the search space, but a limit that is too strict can exclude the correct road before a sequence is evaluated. The two-road teaching model above does not implement candidate pruning or speed-based constraints; it demonstrates observation uncertainty and transition consistency only.

Is the estimate accurate and available enough for the task?

Choose requirements for the actual service: fleet tracking, emergency response, charging or vehicle control. Compare the estimated position with an independent reference trajectory; record wrong-road choices, physical errors, outages, recovery time and computational delay. Check whether reported uncertainty agrees with observed error. Correctly identifying a road does not establish lane-level accuracy. Historical experiments and announcements apply to their stated equipment and conditions. For example, LTA’s 23 October 2023 ERP 2.0 announcement described distance-charging capability while retaining the charging framework and stating there was no immediate plan to introduce distance-based charging.

Capability and adopted policy need separate evidence.
