Digital Transformation of Modern Supply Chains

Digital Transformation of Modern Supply Chains

Lecture 01 · 15 September 2025 · Course instructor: Prof. Wai Kin (Victor) Chan

Lecture overview

This lecture connects the development of supply chains and artificial intelligence with the practical decisions that logistics organizations must make. It examines the data, skills, workflows and responsibility needed to use AI, then follows three cases: forecasting demand and choosing purchases for fresh food; coordinating shipment information through Flexport; and testing network plans with S.F. Holding’s digital twin. The cases distinguish predicting what may happen from deciding what to do. They also show why useful automation depends on people, shared information and operational constraints.

Lecturer profile

Wai Kin (Victor) Chan is a professor at Tsinghua University and the course instructor. His research includes simulation, optimization and statistical analysis, with applications in transportation and other complex systems.

Official Tsinghua SIGS profile

Student synthesis

This section brings together anonymized coursework from the Fall 2025 class. We reviewed the submitted responses, compared the ideas raised, and grouped recurring themes while preserving meaningful differences in perspective. Student names and IDs are not shown. The findings describe the submitted work, rather than the views of every student or a measured outcome of the lecture.

The two-page report asked students to identify three things they liked, suggest three improvements, list 3–10 keywords, and explain smart logistics and the role of AI using the lecture and those keywords.

Q1 — What did students appreciate about the lecture?

What students said

  • The fresh-food, Flexport and S.F. Holding cases helped students connect general ideas to purchasing, shipment coordination and network operations.
  • The history of supply chains and AI gave students a way to place newer technologies in context; others especially valued the discussion of organizational readiness and responsible use.
  • Questions, discussion and live polls made participation part of the learning. One reflection specifically valued a way for quieter students to contribute.

What this suggests. The responses suggest that concrete operating problems and opportunities to discuss them were useful entry points into a broad technical subject.

Q2 — What would students improve or explore further?

What students said

  • Requests for a short technology primer, clearer diagrams and simpler explanations sat alongside requests for more mathematics, model selection and deeper case analysis.
  • Students suggested brief group problems, guided platform activities, demonstrations and more time to discuss implementation. Suggested follow-ups included smaller firms, financial returns, security and workforce transition.
  • Pace preferences differed. Several students wanted tighter scope or more time for selected cases, while one wanted a faster overall pace. Advance materials and short recaps were proposed to help connect the sections.
  • Access also mattered: a reflection reported difficulty reaching the S.F. platform, and another asked for English alternatives to Chinese-only preparation and better audibility for student contributions.

What this suggests. A short core explanation with optional technical detail and a focused practical activity would respond to these different needs without assuming that everyone wanted the same pace or depth.

Q3 — Which keywords did students associate with smart logistics?

What students said

  • The lists combined technologies—automation, AI, IoT, big data, digital twins and blockchain—with operating capabilities such as visibility, forecasting, connectivity and optimization.
  • Sustainability and resilience appeared alongside efficiency. Less frequent terms, including serving people, modularity, effectiveness and decision weights, widened the idea of what makes a system “smart”.
  • The families overlap: a response may connect sensors with a digital twin, or AI with optimization. A keyword mention records vocabulary used in the report, not endorsement, competence or a vote on the most important technology.

What this suggests. Taken together, the vocabulary describes a connected operating system rather than a single device or algorithm.

Q4 — What is a smart logistics system, and how might AI change it?

What students said

  • Students commonly described connected people, processes and data: information about demand or movement informs forecasts, plans and adjustments across the supply chain. Some explicitly included physical, information and financial flows.
  • Many envisaged more adaptive routing, forecasting and automated operations. Their expectations for autonomy differed: some imagined extensive automation, while others emphasized human judgment, exceptions and phased delegation.
  • Sustainability was often associated with less waste and better resource use. One reflection also asked readers to consider the energy, water and materials used by AI, together with workforce transition and barriers for smaller firms.
  • Several responses judged technology by the service it enables. Distinctive ideas included simpler IT before AI, explicit decision weights, and the difference between using resources efficiently and actually meeting a service goal.

What this suggests. The synthesis points toward a practical definition: a smart logistics system connects information to feasible decisions and checks whether those decisions improve the intended service. This is an editorial interpretation of the responses, not a consensus definition or a forecast.

Teaching interpretation

How information reaches an operating decision

A forecast, a shared shipment record and a simulated network plan each support a different decision. This optional six-stage explanation connects those roles across the three lecture cases; it was constructed for the archive, rather than quoted from the lecturer.

Original assignment wording

The assignment asked for three appreciated features; these are recurring themes in those answers, not a satisfaction score or ranking.

Students did not all emphasize the same part. Some focused on the historical storyline, some on a particular company or application, and others on ethics, investment priorities or the classroom discussion.

Praise for clear or accessible presentation coexists with Q2 comments about dense slides and small diagrams. The two observations concern different experiences and should not be reduced to a single verdict.

Q2 — What would students improve or explore further?

Original assignment wording

List 3 things that can be improved in this lecture?

These suggestions come from Q2. They are not moved into the list of what students liked in Q1.

One reflection proposed a faster pace and brief brainstorming; another preferred moving quickly through basic material to spend more time on predictive-model choices. Other responses asked for more time or less slide content.

Some asked for accessible definitions of digital twins and stochastic programming, while others wanted the mathematics behind those methods. A layered explanation is an editorial response to that tension, not a teaching method reported as tested by the class.

The platform-access, language and microphone comments describe individual reported difficulties. They do not establish that every student experienced them.

Q4 — What is a smart logistics system, and how might AI change it?

Original assignment wording

Reflect on the lecture PPT and your list of keywords. Describe your understanding of what a smart logistic system is, and how AI can shape the evolution of logistic systems.

Q4 asked for both an understanding of the system and the possible role of AI in its evolution. Future scenarios alone would omit the first half of the assignment.

Some responses imagined almost fully automated networks; others retained human responsibility for safety, empathy, ethics or unexpected conditions. These differing outlooks are preserved as student perspectives, not claims about what will happen.

A response argued that basic information systems can solve many logistics problems without AI. Another distinguished efficiency from effectiveness; another called for explicit criteria and weights when delegating decisions.

Claims about universal AI gains, historical case percentages, regulation or particular future services are not established by reflections. The external readings offer separate context and should not be presented as student measurements.

Coordination across partners

Modular tools and cross-border services still need compatible data and reliable handoffs. This preserves a specific student perspective about integration alongside the different views on autonomy.

Distinctive student perspectives

How information reaches an operating decision

Stages may overlap or repeat in practice. Sensing and connecting improve the information available; prediction estimates uncertain conditions; optimization compares feasible actions; execution assigns work; review checks outcomes against a baseline.

A useful decision can also be supported by a simpler information system. The diagram does not imply that AI is required at every stage.

Check clear responsibilities, staff skills and exception handling; make service, cost and decision priorities explicit; and test capacity, uncertainty, safety and data quality.
