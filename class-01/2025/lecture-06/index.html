Driving roles and responsibility

Start with who performs the driving task and who must respond if the operating conditions change.

SAE’s six levels, from 0 to 5, classify a driving feature and its roles. “L2+” is an industry term rather than another SAE level. An automated driving system (ADS) performs the driving task at the automation levels described below; the level itself does not certify safety.

Feature roles · brief paraphrases of the SAE taxonomy
LevelNameRole while engaged
L0No driving automationDriver performs the task; warnings or brief interventions may assist.
L1Driver assistanceSustained steering OR speed support; driver supervises.
L2Partial driving automationSustained steering AND speed support; driver supervises.
L3Conditional driving automationADS performs the task in its domain; a fallback-ready user must respond when required.
L4High driving automationADS performs the task and fallback within its domain; no driver takeover is required.
L5Full driving automationADS performs the task and fallback across driver-manageable on-road conditions.

Read SAE J3016’s 2021 taxonomy ↗

Compare a feature within and near its limits

Choose a feature and an operating situation. This example assumes it is validly engaged within its operational design domain (ODD), which may limit roads, speed, weather or lighting.

At L3, a fallback-ready user must respond when intervention is required. At L4, the ADS handles fallback, potentially reaching a minimal risk condition. The response depends on the situation; a mapped zone or remote assistance alone does not establish the level.

To understand a feature, check its required driver role. For example, Tesla’s FSD (Supervised) documentation requires active driver supervision. A separate robotaxi service would need evidence about its own feature and operating conditions.

2025 STUDENT PERSPECTIVES / 02 OF 05LECTURE 06

From sensing to control

Once the roles are clear, follow how a vehicle turns observations into motion. Keep live control distinct from the work of improving its models.

01

INPUT → DECISION → CHECK

Compare sensor strengths and weaknesses

Students compare camera detail, radar range and relative motion, LiDAR geometry and short-range ultrasonic sensing. Satellite positioning and inertial measurements help estimate vehicle state. Weather, lighting, obstructions, calibration, computation and cost affect performance; fusion needs testing in those conditions.

Validate updates before putting them into operation

Model development collects relevant events, checks and labels data, trains candidates, validates them and releases updates. A trip does not automatically rewrite the live controller. Fleet operations also need monitoring, maintenance and incident response.

Collect the situations that matter.

The lecture compares vision-focused, multisensor, ecosystem and supplier strategies. Students connect them with data quality and hardware integration. Fleet size and computing throughput need context about routes, software and test conditions before they can support performance comparisons.

The five steps repeat as a feedback loop, with uncertainty in perception and prediction. Modular and end-to-end systems can organize the functions differently. Simulated scenarios add test coverage; real-world evaluation needs to check how closely they represent actual conditions.

Connected-city services

Beyond one vehicle, students propose ways to coordinate transport services. Examine which decisions each connection supports.

Vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V), infrastructure (V2I), pedestrian (V2P) and network (V2N) links can provide different information. Messages need to be timely, authentic and usable across systems; missing or inconsistent data needs a defined response.

Traffic flow and service capacity

Connected services make capacity promises. Try two small calculations to see how much the result depends on the assumptions.

Students discuss mixed traffic, local and network control, campus shuttles and freight services. The examples below isolate two capacity questions; they do not simulate the full network.

Change the assumed crossing headway

For this single-lane example, human-driven vehicles cross 2.0 seconds apart. Adjust the share of automated vehicles and their assumed crossing headway.

MEAN CROSSING HEADWAY
IDEALIZED FLOW
VS HUMAN-ONLY ASSUMPTION

Mean headway h̄ = (1 − p) × 2.0 + p × hAV; flow q = 3600/h̄ vehicles/hour. The example assumes continuous demand and averages the assigned headways. It omits following-pair behavior, disturbances, lane changes, signals and safety constraints. It calculates an idealized flow rather than a validated capacity or a recommended following gap.

Check the fleet needed for a departure interval

A student shuttle proposal motivates this example. The illustrative cycle includes travel, stops, recovery and assigned charging time. Count only vehicles available for service.

MINIMUM AVAILABLE FLEET
MEETS INTERVAL TARGET?

Departure interval = cycle time / available vehicles; required fleet = ceil(cycle time / target interval). The example assumes equal spacing and identical cycles without disruption. Reserve vehicles, staffing, passenger capacity, accessibility, variable stops and battery scheduling require further checks.

Test the control strategy

A ring-road field experiment shows a controlled vehicle reducing stop-and-go waves in its test setting. To compare a wider network, define demand, driver behavior, routing, controls and evaluation measures.

2025 STUDENT PERSPECTIVES / 05 OF 05LECTURE 06

People, trust and evidence

Technical capability and capacity are part of deployment. Students also ask how the service feels, who can use it and how it will be evaluated.

PERSONAL EXPERIENCE

Review passenger comfort

One student recalls abrupt braking on a commuting shuttle. This raises questions about comfort, false detections and vehicle behavior. Compare unnecessary braking with near misses, injuries and passenger feedback; one experience does not establish a fleet-wide rate.

ACCESS + PARTICIPATION

Make the whole journey accessible

Students raise affordability, smartphone access, wheelchair access and multilingual support. Include boarding, assistance, accessible stops, emergency communication and recovery alongside booking.

PATROL + HUMAN AUTHORITY

Define who reviews and acts on an alert

Patrol proposals raise false alarms, bias and authority to intervene. Driving performance and threat classification need separate evaluation. Specify who checks an alert and authorizes a response.

TRUST + EXPLANATIONS

Check explanations against event records

Some students ask for reassuring passenger explanations, including those generated by language models. Check whether an explanation matches the event logs, acknowledges uncertainty and gives passengers a way to reach human support.

ENERGY + URBAN SPACE

Evaluate energy and traffic effects

Students imagine cleaner fleets, smoother travel and less parking. Compare electricity sources, occupancy, empty repositioning, total distance and the trips being replaced. Electric propulsion, shared use and automation each affect the result differently.

WORKFORCE + OPERATIONS

Budget for the supporting work

Students discuss maintenance, monitoring, simulation and safety roles alongside changing driving jobs. A service budget also needs charging, insurance and incident support, with a plan for staffing and training.

APPLICATION BOUNDARIES

Evaluate each service in its own setting

Robotaxis, ports, mines, delivery and campus patrol involve different people, equipment and conditions. A controlled site may simplify some interactions, while still needing checks for failures and change.

Design a pilot around a clear question.

Define the operating domain, hazards, fallback, maintenance and incident response. Compare safety outcomes per exposure, reliability, uncomfortable braking, interventions, total costs and access with a suitable baseline. Include privacy, data retention and recovery from network loss.

Simulation coverage and disengagement counts describe parts of a test program. Read Waymo’s safety materials with their operating area, exposure, methods and comparator in view before drawing a comparison.
