Autonomous driving

Smart city transportation and autonomous driving

Lecture 06 · 27 October 2025 · Lecturer: Prof. Bokui Chen

Lecture overview

Bokui Chen’s lecture connects autonomous-driving technology with transport services. The supplied deck introduces driver/system roles, sensors and fusion, the steps from sensing to vehicle control, fleet data development, vehicle-to-everything (V2X) communication and vehicle operations. It compares industry approaches and applications in robotaxis, public transit, freight, ports, mines, delivery and campus services, then raises adoption and trust questions. The syllabus also planned microscopic traffic simulation and mixed traffic; the supplied deck does not establish that those models were taught in detail. The traffic and shuttle calculators below are later teaching examples, with explicit assumptions.

Lecturer profile

Bokui Chen is an associate professor at Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School. He is the lecturer named for this session in the Fall 2025 syllabus and on the supplied autonomous-driving deck.

Student synthesis

This section brings together anonymized coursework from the Fall 2025 class. We reviewed the submitted responses, compared the ideas raised, and grouped recurring themes while preserving meaningful differences in perspective. Student names and IDs are not shown. The findings describe the submitted work, rather than the views of every student or a measured outcome of the lecture.

Which smart transportation application did students introduce?

Students were asked to write a two-page report introducing a smart transportation application. The reports range from existing-service studies to new service proposals and broader analyses of autonomous driving.

Connecting a traveler with a workable service

One report studies Yahoo! Transit through journey time, fares and transfers. Other students propose platforms such as CAMP and SmartFleet that combine rides, buses, delivery, dispatch or charging. These proposals emphasize coordination between the vehicle, local infrastructure and a fleet platform; they do not establish deployment or measured savings.

Freight and shuttle ideas need an operating plan

Application reports propose freight platoons, a campus–metro shuttle and parcel consolidation through a temporary microhub. Students discuss spacing, schedules, charging, maintenance and coordination across organizations. Their service targets make useful questions for a pilot, but are not observed performance results.

Autonomous operation requires more than a sensor list

Reports compare vision-focused and multisensor approaches, then connect perception with prediction, planning and control. They also discuss data collection, model validation and fleet monitoring. Students often favored complementary sensors, while some framed the choice as a trade-off involving cost, power, failure modes and the evidence needed for the application.

Who can use the service, and who answers when it fails?

Students raise affordability, smartphone access, accessible boarding, privacy and human support. One recalls abrupt braking on a shuttle; patrol reports question false alarms, bias and the authority to intervene. Some welcome passenger explanations, including language-model interfaces, while also discussing trust, liability and public participation.

The reports suggest evaluating a specific service rather than autonomy in the abstract: identify the users, operating conditions, responsibilities and desired outcome, then test it against a suitable baseline. The examples below separate driving roles, traffic arithmetic and fleet scheduling.

Original lecture assignment

Write a 2-page report to introduce a smart transportation application.

The theme headings organize the submitted work; they are not additional assignment questions.

Freight and shuttle ideas need an operating plan

The shuttle proposal specifies a route, stops, eight starter vehicles, three-minute peak departures, charging, a staged pilot and reliability targets. A timetable still needs a measured cycle time and enough vehicles actually available at each moment. The freight-platoon report raises coordinated braking, cut-ins, connectivity, maintenance and cross-border rules. The CityFlow microhub concept proposes combining parcels from several carriers for cargo-bike delivery; its percentage savings are scenario claims without a measured baseline. It is a student concept, distinct from the similarly named traffic simulator.

Mixed-traffic discussion in another report considers ring/grid models and local versus network routing, but its thresholds were not verified by the supplied deck.

Autonomous operation requires more than a sensor list

Some reports present sensor fusion as a route to stronger robustness; a more qualified comparison considers hardware costs and how failures will be tested. The lecture’s five steps are a simplified functional loop, not a mandatory implementation architecture. Fleet data can support model development, but a collected event does not automatically improve or rewrite a live controller. Data checking, candidate training, validation and controlled release are separate from current vehicle control. SAE levels classify driving features and required roles; they are not safety certificates, an inevitable sequence of upgrades or proof that a vehicle without a visible driver is L4.

Who can use the service, and who answers when it fails?

The access discussion includes people who cannot drive or use a smartphone, and the full journey rather than booking alone. A personal shuttle experience raises comfort questions but is not a fleet-wide braking rate. The patrol reports distinguish moving a vehicle from correctly classifying a threat and deciding who may act. Generated explanations can reassure passengers, yet must be checked against event records and uncertainty. The reports also connect adoption with maintenance, monitoring, training and workforce changes. Cleaner travel or reduced parking are aspirations that require evidence about occupancy, empty travel, energy sources and the trips displaced.

Connected transport proposals and their evidence

The Yahoo! Transit report provides a concrete traveler decision: compare routes that differ in time, cost and transfers. The official service verifies route searches, timetables and operating information, but not the report’s claims about an internal AI algorithm, language coverage, popularity or congestion reduction. CAMP and SmartFleet are student concepts. One report develops Apollo Connect and Traffic Pulse as complementary proposals. Their vehicle/edge/cloud and shared-observation ideas would require rules for data freshness, access, privacy and failure handling.

Vehicle-to-vehicle, infrastructure, pedestrian and network links carry different information. Their messages need timely, authentic and interoperable handling. A bus-priority request is one input to signal control, with pedestrian and other traffic constraints still relevant. The USDOT pilot briefing in Resources reports evaluations specific to its sites and conditions.

Who performs driving and fallback when conditions change?

The dynamic driving task covers vehicle motion and response to road events. At L0–L2 the driver performs that task and supervises any support feature. L1 supports either sustained steering or speed; L2 supports both while the driver supervises. At L3 the ADS performs the task within its domain, with a fallback-ready user expected to respond when required. At L4 the ADS performs both the task and fallback within its domain, without depending on passenger takeover; fallback may bring it to a minimal risk condition.

L5 extends the driving and fallback roles across driver-manageable on-road conditions. An operating domain can limit roads, speed, weather or other conditions. L2+, incident logging and a driverless appearance are not additional SAE levels. Check each feature’s actual instructions and evidence.

Feature roles · brief paraphrases of the SAE taxonomy
Level Name Role while engaged
L0 No driving automation Driver performs the task; warnings or brief interventions may assist.
L1 Driver assistance Sustained steering OR speed support; driver supervises.
L2 Partial driving automation Sustained steering AND speed support; driver supervises.
L3 Conditional driving automation ADS performs the task in its domain; a fallback-ready user must respond when required.
L4 High driving automation ADS performs the task and fallback within its domain; no driver takeover is required.
L5 Full driving automation ADS performs the task and fallback across driver-manageable on-road conditions.

How a vehicle turns observations into motion

Perception and localization estimate a scene from sensors. Prediction retains plausible actions of other road users rather than treating an inferred intention as certain. Planning selects a feasible path and speed profile; control executes and monitors them as new observations arrive. Calibration, timing and uncertainty affect the whole loop. Model development is separate: relevant events are collected, data checked, candidates trained and validated, and approved changes released. Simulation contributes coverage but still needs correspondence with real conditions. Fleet operations need maintenance, monitoring and incident response.

Cameras contribute image detail for signs, markings and objects; radar supplies range and relative motion, while LiDAR measures geometry and ultrasonic sensors support short-range detection. Satellite and inertial measurements help estimate vehicle state. Lighting, weather, obstructions, calibration, computation and cost affect these contributions. Sensor diversity alone does not prove robustness.

How does an assumed headway change idealized traffic flow?

Let p be automated share as a fraction and hAV its assigned headway in seconds. The model averages assigned crossing headways: h̄ = (1 − p)×2.0 + p×hAV. It then computes q = 3600/h̄ vehicles/hour and change = (2.0/h̄ − 1)×100%. At p = 0.5 and hAV = 1.2 s, h̄ = 1.6 s and q = 2,250 vehicles/hour, 25% above the assumed human-only baseline. A larger automated share helps only when its assumed headway is shorter; at 2.0 s there is no arithmetic change, and above 2.0 s flow falls.

The inputs are illustrative, not measurements or recommendations. Continuous demand, homogeneous assigned headways and a single stream are assumed. Following-pair behavior, stop-and-go dynamics, queues, lane changes, signals, network routing, pedestrian interactions and safety constraints are absent. Stern et al.’s ring-road field experiment and SUMO documentation offer further study of dynamics, distinct from this calculator.

How many available shuttles are needed for the departure interval?

For cycle time C minutes, N available vehicles and target interval H minutes, ideal interval = C/N and required fleet = ceil(C/H). The available fleet meets the arithmetic target when C/N ≤ H. At the defaults C = 30 min, N = 8 and H = 3 min, the interval is 3.75 min and the required fleet is 10: two more available vehicles, before any reserve allowance. Corresponding theoretical frequency is 60/(C/N) departures/hour; this is 16 at the default fleet and 20 at the target interval.

Vehicles unavailable for charging, maintenance or faults should not be counted in N; do not also charge the same downtime twice through C. The arithmetic does not schedule individual batteries or vehicles. Reserve fleet, variability in dwell and travel time, staff, stop access, passenger capacity, crowding and disruption recovery determine whether a real service can meet the desired interval. The student’s eight-vehicle/three-minute proposal motivates checking the relationship but supplies no measured 30-minute cycle.

What evidence would justify starting or expanding this service?

Define the operating domain, expected users, hazards and fallback arrangements. Choose measures for the claim being tested: safety outcomes per exposure, uncomfortable braking, service reliability, wait time, passenger capacity, full costs and accessibility. Compare the results with a suitable baseline and include uncertainty. Assess maintenance, charging, incident support, data retention and loss of connectivity. A signal-priority request remains an input to a constrained traffic-control decision; it does not remove pedestrian conflicts. Company safety materials apply to their stated routes, exposure and methods.

A market forecast, a large fleet, simulated miles, an incident log or a fluent generated explanation alone does not validate the service.

Accessibility covers boarding, accessible stops, assistance, emergency communication and recovery, alongside booking. Patrol systems also need a separate evaluation of threat classification, false alarms and human authority to intervene. Environmental claims should account for electricity sources, occupancy, empty repositioning and the trips replaced. A service budget includes monitoring, training, charging, insurance and incident support.
