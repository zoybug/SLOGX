Urban drone logistics

Introduction to Instant Delivery with Drones

Lecture 04 · 13 October 2025 · Lecturer: Prof. Xinlei Chen

Lecture overview

Xinlei Chen examines instant delivery as a coordinated service, using Meituan’s urban drone operation as the main example. A customer order passes through merchant preparation, a rider’s ground handoff, station loading, flight and collection at a designated receiving point. Positioning and perception support the aircraft, while charging sites, remote supervision, maintenance and the TBOS management framework support the fleet. The lecture then considers response delay, route and time-slot coordination, fault-tolerant control, information security, noise and privacy. A fast flight is therefore only one part of providing a useful and dependable delivery service.

Lecturer profile

Xinlei Chen is an associate professor at Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School. His research includes intelligent Internet of Things, multi-agent collaboration, pervasive computing, robotics and drone swarms. The supplied lecture introduces his work on low-latency response and collaborative aerial and ground sensing.

Student synthesis

This section brings together anonymized coursework from the Fall 2025 class. We reviewed the submitted responses, compared the ideas raised, and grouped recurring themes while preserving meaningful differences in perspective. Student names and IDs are not shown. The findings describe the submitted work, rather than the views of every student or a measured outcome of the lecture.

Assignment — What did students think about drone delivery?

Students wrote a two-page report giving their views of drone delivery. The brief suggested five aspects—benefits and shortcomings, delivery process, urban technical challenges, current-system problems and improvements—while allowing other topics. The headings below follow those suggested aspects; they were not five compulsory questions. Preparation also included talks on drone delivery and trying Meituan’s service.

Benefits and shortcomings — Where could drones add value?

What students said

  • Students valued avoiding road congestion and reaching places that are difficult to serve by road. Several saw particular value in urgent medicines, samples or small time-sensitive deliveries.
  • Payload, range, battery use and weather narrowed the applications they considered feasible. Some welcomed routine meal delivery; others thought urgent medical or business deliveries offered a clearer reason to bear the infrastructure cost.
  • Convenience was contested. Faster flight could be offset by walking to a receiving point, a limited choice of merchants or unavailable flights when customers most wanted delivery.

What this suggests: The responses suggest comparing the value of time for a particular service with the complete delivery’s constraints, rather than treating drones as a general replacement for ground transport.

Delivery process — What happens around the flight?

What students said

  • Many reports followed the lecture’s hybrid relay: order and merchant preparation, rider transport to a dispatch station, loading and flight, then customer collection at a designated receiving point.
  • Students identified work that remains on the ground and in the control centre: package handling, charging, maintenance, remote monitoring and support when an operation goes wrong.
  • Some reports also described another operator’s tether delivery. That receiving arrangement differs from the lecture’s main station-pickup example and should be compared separately.

What this suggests: Automation changes who performs each handoff; it does not make preparation, pickup access or supporting work disappear from the service.

Urban technical challenges — How does the system handle changing conditions?

What students said

  • Reports connected buildings, degraded satellite signals and moving obstacles with the need to combine positioning and perception inputs. Two reflections questioned how sensor updates and processing delay affect the distance travelled before a response.
  • Students discussed both planned coordination and departures from the plan. Shared routes and time slots do not remove the need to recognize deviations, react onboard and coordinate contingencies.
  • A useful distinction in one report was between internal equipment faults and outside disturbances such as airflow or other aircraft. Other responses linked navigation cameras with privacy and noise concerns in residential areas.

What this suggests: Different hazards require different protections: scheduling, sensing, fault tolerance and data governance address related but distinct parts of an urban operation.

Current-system problems — What limits dependable operation?

What students said

  • Students repeatedly returned to ground infrastructure and supporting costs: stations need space, power, maintenance and people, even when one remote pilot can supervise several aircraft.
  • Weather suspension and limited service coverage raised questions about accepted orders and the availability of a ground alternative. Restricted payloads also made larger ground vehicles relevant to the comparison.
  • Reports questioned closed operator systems, shared-airspace coordination and community acceptance. Noise, privacy and responsibility for incidents were concerns alongside flight performance.

What this suggests: A dependable service must be evaluated during interruptions as well as successful flights, using total time, cost and supporting work per completed delivery.

Suggested improvements — What would students test first?

What students said

  • Students proposed improving sensing and processing, batteries and charging, quieter flight, and a usable ground fallback. These proposals address different limits and need their own evaluation.
  • Several favoured staged adoption: urgent medical or industrial deliveries first, or drones serving one leg of a hybrid service with riders, vans or ground robots. Others suggested standardized handoff modules, shared hubs or portable receiving points.
  • Responses also called for clearer coordination between operators, community participation and transparent privacy and incident policies. One identified reflection emphasized training for maintenance and fleet-support roles as work changes.

What this suggests: The proposals provide questions for a pilot, rather than validated forecasts. Each should be tested against actual demand, access, reliability, costs and the people affected.

Quiz

Twelve practice questions apply the lecture’s handoff, response-time and service-evaluation concepts; they do not affect course grades.

Benefits depend on the service and its users

Original assignment wording (verbatim): Write a 2-page report about your think of delivery with drones. You can include the following contents but not limited to them: What are the benefits and shortcomings of drone delivery? What is the process of drone delivery? What are the technical challenges of drone delivery in urban scenarios? What are problems of current drone delivery systems? Do you have any suggestions on the current drone delivery system?

The first suggested report aspect asked about benefits and shortcomings. Reports repeatedly contrasted bypassing road traffic with limited loads, energy reserves and weather-dependent availability. These are students’ explanations and expectations, not measurements of a common service trial.

One response regarded healthcare and emergency access as more consequential than ordinary e-commerce novelty. Another proposed beginning with business-to-business samples or spare parts between controlled endpoints. In contrast, other reports emphasized the convenience of quick meals. The synthesis retains these different assessments of where speed has value.

A report that discussed designated pickup pointed out the customer’s walk and reduced merchant choice. A separate response noted that demand can rise during poor weather even when drones cannot fly. These observations make accessibility and availability relevant to comparing services.

Electric propulsion was often described as an environmental benefit. This is a claim to evaluate, not an established result of the reports. Electricity generation, batteries, supporting hubs and the matched ground alternative affect life-cycle comparisons. The 2018 Stolaroff study is independent research with its own aircraft and deployment assumptions.

Handoffs, staffing and different receiving designs

The second suggested aspect asked for the drone delivery process. The supplied deck depicts an app order, merchant preparation, a rider carrying the meal to the delivery station, loading onto the drone and customer collection. Its later order-taking diagram also checks whether the merchant and order are eligible for drone delivery.

One reflection explains why a predictable station is easier to automate than a busy restaurant pickup area. Others list loading staff, remote pilots, emergency support and maintenance. The deck’s pilot-to-aircraft ratios describe supervision, not the total labour needed for a completed order.

Order-to-collection time can include preparation, rider travel, station queues, flight and the customer’s walk. These durations are different from flight duration, and not every report describes them with the same boundary.

Some responses blend station pickup with a tether mechanism. Wing’s 2022 company explanation describes lowering a package while its aircraft remains airborne. That is a useful alternative architecture, but it should not be attributed to every operator or treated as the receiving method in the supplied Meituan lecture example.

The stage explorer is an editorial explanation of the service. Eligibility and recovery checks extend the lecture’s main diagram so readers can examine exceptions; they are not observed timings or additional assignment questions.

Sensing, delay, coordination and security

The third suggested aspect asked about urban technical challenges. The deck includes multimodal navigation, visual positioning, scene understanding, fault-tolerant control, parachutes and a cloud–edge management architecture. Reflections use these ideas to discuss degraded signals, changing obstacles and many aircraft sharing space.

One response distinguishes internal failure from aerodynamic interactions and airflow around buildings. Additional backup equipment can address particular equipment failures; it does not by itself resolve every environmental disturbance.

Two reflections give inconsistent explanations of 10 m/s flight with 10 Hz sensing. A periodic 10 Hz update has a 0.1 s interval, so the worst-case wait for the next sample corresponds to 1 m at that speed. A larger distance needs additional delays or other assumptions. The response-time exercise makes those assumptions explicit instead of adopting an ambiguous reported figure.

The lecture’s TBOS diagram distinguishes route/time planning, conflict detection and resolution, monitoring and environmental information. An unexpected deviation still requires timely detection and a workable contingency response. NASA’s UTM project provides a historical US research comparison, not local operating permission or proof that a particular urban service is safe.

The lecture’s information-security layers include personnel/account controls, hardware and navigation protection, encrypted communications, and area-based control with emergency priorities. These address different threats and should not be condensed into a claim that encryption alone ensures safety.

A reflection proposes less intrusive sensing but cautions that obstacle detection must still work. Privacy review therefore needs the data purpose, access and retention as well as the performance of the perception method. Wing’s company explanation describes its own design; it is not independent evidence about every camera-equipped drone.

Availability, costs and evidence for service claims

The fourth suggested aspect concerned problems in current systems. Reports discussed substantial research and infrastructure investment, continuing human oversight, charging and battery logistics, and coordination across operators. These are reported concerns; the coursework does not establish a measured cost or adoption rate.

A pilot-to-aircraft ratio covers one role. The supplied deck separately lists chief, remote and emergency pilots, ground-site management, maintenance, comprehensive support and training. Comparing labour costs requires a consistent boundary that includes the work still needed around the flight.

Delivery totals and historical average-time claims in the deck describe their original settings. They do not establish a current incident rate or guarantee service for every order. Safety comparisons need exposure, incident counts, severity and operating conditions; cost comparisons need the same service and demand boundary.

A useful pilot would record eligible and completed orders, order-to-collection times, queue and handoff durations, weather suspensions, fallback completion, incidents, complaints, staff hours and total costs. This is an editorial evaluation plan drawn from the questions raised, not data collected by the class.

Reports contain varying payload, flight-range and permission claims. Those figures are not used as universal technical specifications or current legal rules. Aircraft, route, operating conditions and jurisdiction need to be specified before adopting them.

Proposals, disagreements and workforce transition

The fifth suggested aspect invited improvements. A response focused on faster perception and standardized merchant handoffs; another proposed shared multi-operator infrastructure. A third suggested portable pickup points to adapt coverage. These ideas differ in who must provide, maintain and govern the infrastructure.

The proposed medical/business-first path prioritizes deliveries where delay has clear consequences and endpoints can be controlled. Reports enthusiastic about everyday convenience envisage a broader market. The synthesis does not turn those differing priorities into a class preference or predict which one will succeed.

Several responses propose combining drones with riders, vans or ground robots, particularly for portions of the journey drones cannot conveniently serve. A hybrid arrangement still needs synchronized handoffs, enough fallback capacity and a realistic customer promise.

One reflection expects a gradual transition towards technical and fleet-support jobs and calls for training. A separate companion essay raises displacement and unequal gains. Possible new roles do not establish that affected riders will receive accessible training, comparable pay or employment.

Suggestions about solar assistance, future batteries, universal drone corridors or doorstep service are proposals. They are not capabilities verified by the supplied lecture or forecasts established by the submitted work.

Response-time definitions and calculation

For periodic sensor updates at frequency f in Hz, the worst-case wait for the next sample is 1/f seconds, or 1000/f milliseconds. This is a sampling interval, not a measured processing delay or the average wait at a randomly arriving event.

The model adds worst-case sampling wait, processing delay, communication delay and actuation delay. Total pre-response delay in seconds is the sum in milliseconds divided by 1000. Distance before response is constant speed in m/s multiplied by that delay in seconds.

The full-chain preset uses 10 m/s, 10 Hz, 200 ms processing, 100 ms communication and 100 ms actuation. Total delay is 500 ms (0.50 s), giving 5.00 m before response. Sampling alone contributes 1.00 m. At 20 Hz with the other delays unchanged, the wait falls to 50 ms: total delay is 450 ms and distance is 4.50 m.

The Sampling only preset sets processing, communication and actuation to zero, leaving 0.10 s and 1.00 m at 10 Hz and 10 m/s. Sliders can then vary speed, frequency and processing; the preset determines the communication and actuation values.

The quantities are chosen teaching inputs, not measured performance of the lecture’s aircraft. Braking distance, acceleration, obstacle-motion prediction, tracking error, missed samples, queueing and delay variation are omitted. Neither a smaller calculated distance nor a high sample frequency alone establishes collision avoidance or a safe operating margin.
