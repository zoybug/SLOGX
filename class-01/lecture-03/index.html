Lecture 03 / 29 September 2026

Intelligent Warehousing Systems

Follow a tote from receiving to a picking station. A storage choice made at the dock can determine how far a worker travels, how many bins a robot moves and whether an order reaches packing in time.

LecturerProf. Peng Yang

Peng Yang is an associate professor at Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School. His research covers robotic warehousing, AI in warehousing and logistics, dynamic order picking, and sustainable logistics. These interests connect the lecture’s equipment comparisons with decisions about how warehouses are operated.

The assignment

Student work

Write a two-page reflection on what you learned. The brief invited students to explore how the warehouse works, how to choose a system, which operating decisions to optimize, and what a real deployment reveals about benefits, limits and future development.

How the work was read

The themes draw on original, supplemental and late warehousing reflections. The four prompts were suggested ways to approach one assignment. The summaries omit names, identifiers and grades.

Read the 2025 class on intelligent warehousing

The next pick starts at receiving

Reports followed the item and its inventory record through receiving, put-away and picking.

Read the analysis

Students began with the moment a delivery is scanned. The item has arrived, but a picker still needs to know where it was put. Recording both the item and its storage location lets the warehouse management system issue a retrieval task when an order arrives. Reports on goods-to-person systems followed that task one step further. A robot can bring a tote or shelf to a workstation, where a person checks the item and picks the required quantity. Other reflections stayed with manual operations and asked what better records and pick lists could do for a worker walking the aisles. Both accounts depend on the recorded location matching the physical one.

Choose the equipment for the order mix

Small, frequent orders, bulky items and seasonal peaks led students to different equipment choices.

Read the analysis

Students planning for many small orders considered bringing shelves or totes to a picker to reduce repeated walking. Reports on dense grids asked how often the bin you need would sit below another one. Floor area and clear height could favour a compact installation, while bulky, fragile or refrigerated goods changed what it could handle. Demand also changed the investment decision. Capacity bought for a promotion might sit idle on an ordinary day. Some reports favoured adding robots or upgrading a manual operation in stages. Their comparisons included the costs of fitting software to existing records, training staff and keeping the equipment running.

When can a batch afford to wait?

Picking two nearby orders together can save a trip, but waiting for the second uses time on the first.

Read the analysis

Reports described sending a worker or robot on one trip for orders that share an aisle or a product. Some extended the idea to orders still arriving. They considered holding a current order briefly if another compatible order was likely to appear. That decision depends on the release time and the shipping deadline. Waiting can leave too little time to pick, check and pack an urgent order, even when the eventual route is shorter. Students also discussed zoning and the sequence of work at stations. A batch that cuts travel can still deliver several totes to an already busy picker at once. The measure they wanted to improve therefore matters.

A free slot can hide a future move

Students connected the put-away decision with reshuffling, retrieval time and traffic in busy aisles.

Read the analysis

Several reflections looked ahead from the place where an arriving bin is stored. In a stacked system, placing another bin above it saves space now and can create an extra move when the lower bin is requested. Keeping frequently requested products near a picking station can reduce travel, but concentrating work in one area also brings workers and robots to the same access point. Students linked these choices to routing, replenishment and the number of stations available. A practical comparison suggested by their reasoning is to give two storage rules the same orders and count both retrieval moves and orders ready for dispatch.

Who handles the exceptions?

Company cases brought damaged goods, downtime and the cost of human support into the discussion.

Read the analysis

Reports on operators and equipment suppliers described people checking orders, packing fragile products and handling stock that a machine could not pick. Students considering wider automation also counted the technicians, training and maintenance the new equipment would require. A fleet depends on reliable communication and accurate stock records. A failure in a shared system can interrupt several workstations at once. Other reflections used supplier figures on storage density or picking speed to explain why a company might invest. Those figures concern particular sites and measures. A team assessing another site would still need to know its product mix, the comparison period and who keeps orders moving during an interruption.

Teaching example

Try the decision

Release now or wait for a batch?

Order A is due at minute 9. Picking it alone takes 4 minutes. Picking it alongside a later order takes 5. Both plans need 3 more minutes to pack A.

Alone, A finishes at minute 7. If the second order arrives at minute 2, the batch finishes at minute 10, one minute late.

Assume an available picker and packing station. The comparison concerns completion time for order A.

Self-check

Decision quiz

Twelve short scenarios. Choose a response and check the reasoning behind it.

A tote was scanned at receiving but its move to a shelf was never recorded. What should the team check first?
Answer and reasoning

The tote’s physical location against the inventory record

A retrieval task depends on the recorded storage location. More equipment cannot identify a tote stored under the wrong location.

A small retailer handles irregular items and has one large seasonal peak. Which comparison would best guide an equipment purchase?
Answer and reasoning

Test manual, hybrid and automated options against ordinary and peak orders, item dimensions and full operating costs

Orders, building constraints and demand variability determine the work the system will actually do. Capacity and speed each describe only part of the choice.

A team stores each arriving bin in the first free position in a stack. Which measure will expose a cost that appears later?
Answer and reasoning

Extra bin moves needed to retrieve requested stock

A quick put-away may bury frequently requested stock. Later retrieval can require moving the bins above it.

Adding robots shortens travel time but leaves completed orders per hour unchanged. What would you measure next?
Answer and reasoning

Queue length and service time at picking and packing stations

Work can accumulate at a station even as travel improves. Queue and service times reveal where order completion is constrained.

An order must be packed by minute 12. Picking takes six minutes and packing takes four. A compatible order might arrive at minute four. Which release rule protects the first order if the second never arrives?
Answer and reasoning

Release the first order by minute two

The first order needs ten minutes of work. Its latest feasible release is minute two, assuming both resources are available. A predicted order cannot extend that deadline.

A manager tests two retrieval schedules for an automated storage system. Which comparison would support a claim of lower energy consumption?
Answer and reasoning

Compare kilowatt-hours for the same retrieval tasks under the same service requirements

Keeping the work and service requirements fixed lets the team test whether sequencing changes the energy required to complete that work.

A robot presents a tote to a packing station. A worker finds that the item inside is damaged. Which design gives the warehouse a dependable way to finish the order?
Answer and reasoning

Provide an exception workflow for the worker to record the damage and request replacement stock

A successful movement does not establish that the item is fit to ship. Recording the exception prevents damaged stock from being treated as available and allows the order to be completed with a replacement.

A case study reports four times as many stored items per square metre after a retrofit. The retailer wants to claim that it now ships four times as many orders per hour. What else would it need?
Answer and reasoning

Comparable before-and-after counts of orders shipped per hour for a similar order mix

Storage density measures use of space. Actual order throughput also depends on retrieval, picking, packing and release capacity.

Two teams have the same picking line. One uses fixed zones. The other lets workers hand off partly completed orders as they meet, shifting where each worker's portion ends. Which observation would distinguish the second policy?
Answer and reasoning

Handoff locations move as the workers' relative pace changes

A bucket-brigade line allows the boundary between workers’ tasks to adjust through handoffs. This can redistribute work when workers move at different speeds.

A chilled-grocery warehouse is considering a batch that reduces picking distance. Some items in the batch would wait at an unrefrigerated packing bench. What condition should the planner check before releasing it?
Answer and reasoning

Whether each item can still meet its permitted time outside chilled storage

Batching can cut travel while extending exposure at a downstream station. The temperature-handling constraint applies throughout the process, including waiting.

Staff are comparing two goods-to-person designs for a shelf of small items. One delivers the entire rack and the other delivers the requested tote. Which observation most directly distinguishes the two systems?
Answer and reasoning

Which storage unit moves from its location to the workstation

The delivery unit distinguishes shelf-to-person from tote-to-person. Either system can maintain inventory records and have a person carry out the final pick.

A retailer is deciding whether a promising pilot should serve the whole building. It worked on an ordinary day. Which further trial would tell the team most about resilience?
Answer and reasoning

Replay a peak order mix with one station unavailable and track missed deadlines and recovery time

Peak demand and a station outage test how the operation redistributes work. Missed deadlines and recovery time show the service consequences for orders.
