Lecture 07 / 3 November 2026

Smart Container Terminal Operations

A container has to leave the ship, cross the terminal and reach a slot in the yard. Zhang followed the decisions behind that journey, from reserving a berth and cranes to releasing vehicles and finding space for the next box.

LecturerProf. Canrong Zhang

Canrong Zhang is a professor at Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School. His research uses operations research and data analysis to study production, logistics and supply chains. His work on joint berth and crane planning appears in the assigned reading.

The assignment

Student work

Write a one-page reflection on what you learned from the lecture. Preparation included a container-terminal video and a research paper on planning berth and quay crane assignments together.

How the work was read

The synthesis covers 32 container-terminal reflections. The lecture’s case-study results retain their original comparison and measure. The summaries omit names, identifiers and grades.

An empty berth can still leave a ship waiting

The reports linked a ship’s berth to the cranes it could use and the distance its containers would travel across the yard.

Read the analysis

Students worked through the difference between finding space at the quay and finding a workable service plan. A vessel may fit at a particular berth, but another vessel may already need the available cranes at that time. A berth far from the export stacks adds travel for the vehicles supplying the ship. Several reflections followed that extra journey back to a waiting crane. Others started with the planning method: list a few feasible combinations of berth, start time and cranes for each ship, then select combinations that can run together. A revised arrival estimate changes those options. The service start time is a decision the terminal can still adjust.

Where does the queue go after unloading?

Faster crane releases can fill the queue at a lock station. Other reports followed the same problem farther inland, to the truck gate.

Read the analysis

In the Tianjin example, students followed a box from the quay crane through a lock station and an intersection to the yard crane. If vehicles reach a busy lock station faster than it can serve them, an increase in crane activity can produce a longer line. Reports considered changing the release time or directing work to another station. Some readers extended the question beyond the lecture model. A yard can keep filling after unloading finishes if trucks cannot collect the boxes. Vessel turnaround, vehicle waiting time and container dwell time measure different parts of that journey. The lock station was the limiting stage in the studied configuration. Another terminal would need its own queue and timing observations.

Which box will have to move twice?

Students used retrieval order and stack height to explain why the closest empty position can be an expensive choice.

Read the analysis

To retrieve a container from the bottom of a yard stack, the crane must first move the containers above it. Several students noticed that choosing a convenient slot for a blocker can bury the very next container on the retrieval list. Comparing two legal destinations therefore takes more than a distance check. A planner also needs the order of retrieval and the height limit at each stack. Some reports approached the problem through onboard stowage across several ports. The shared idea is access from above. The class exercise concerned a yard crane retrieving boxes from land-side stacks, where the available moves and the pickup sequence can be specified directly.

What does a vessel’s arrival tell a customer?

Reports looked beyond the berth to ask when cargo would actually be ready for collection or production.

Read the analysis

Some reflections began with the terminal plan, asking what happens to crane bookings when several vessels arrive close together. Others began with the customer waiting for materials. From that perspective, arriving at port is only the first milestone. The box still needs discharge, any required release and collection from the yard. Those events can have different delays and different owners. Students also discussed the cost of leaving spare capacity or holding stock when arrivals vary. The reports offer reasons a buffer might earn its cost. Choosing its size would require a record of the relevant lead times and the consequence of missing the delivery.

What did the Tianjin comparison measure?

Some students separated the time to finish the last job from congestion along the route. The study tested different scheduling choices for those outcomes.

Read the analysis

Students interpreted two results from the lecture’s case-data tests. In one comparison, the proposed schedule cut makespan, the time until the last job finished, by 18.22% against the rule used as the baseline. A separate optimization step reduced the congestion measure by an average of 65.45% while holding makespan steady. Some reflections kept those comparisons separate. Others discussed whether a slightly longer schedule would be worth accepting if it eased traffic inside the terminal. That is a choice about the operating objective. Applying either result elsewhere would require another comparison with the new terminal’s layout, workload and scheduling rules.

Self-check

Decision quiz

Twelve short scenarios. Choose a response and check the reasoning behind it.

Two ships fit alongside the quay at the same time. Each needs two cranes throughout its proposed service window, and the terminal has three. What must the planner change?
Answer and reasoning

The service windows or a feasible crane allocation

The overlapping plans require four cranes at once. Different berth positions alone leave the same crane shortage.

A quay crane releases boxes faster, but vehicles wait longer at the lock station and the last box finishes at the same time. What should the operator test next?
Answer and reasoning

Stagger releases or use another available lock station

The extra work is accumulating at the next stage. Testing station assignments or release times addresses the queue directly.

A blocker must leave its current stack before the first pickup. Two destinations have room. What distinguishes the move that saves a later relocation?
Answer and reasoning

It leaves the next scheduled pickup accessible

A legal placement can still bury the next box. Look ahead to its pickup before choosing a destination.

A terminal wants to use a congestion result from the Tianjin case to estimate its own gains. Which comparison would support that estimate?
Answer and reasoning

Test both schedules on its own workload and layout using the same congestion measure

The layout, traffic, baseline rule and performance measure all affect the comparison. A result from one case provides a method to test.

A ship has arrived on schedule. The factory wants to send a truck for an import container that needs customs clearance. Which update would establish that pickup can proceed?
Answer and reasoning

The box is available for collection and the required release has been granted

Arrival, discharge and permission to collect are distinct milestones. A truck can still face a hold after the box reaches the yard.

A slide reviewer wants to record the 18.22% and 65.45% figures from the Tianjin example. Which description preserves the comparison?
Answer and reasoning

One test cut makespan by 18.22% against the baseline rule. A separate step cut congestion by 65.45% on average while holding makespan steady.

The first figure concerns the time to finish the last job. The second concerns congestion under a step that preserved the completion time.

Boxes must leave in order 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Box 4 sits on box 1. Two other stacks, each with room for one more box, hold box 2 and box 5. Where can box 4 go with no later relocation?
Answer and reasoning

On box 5, because box 4 leaves before box 5

Placed on box 5, box 4 remains accessible at its scheduled pickup. Placed on box 2, it has to move again before the second pickup.

The terminal limits a vehicle queue to three. A 195-minute schedule creates a peak queue of eight. A 205-minute schedule peaks at two. Which of these plans meets the stated limit?
Answer and reasoning

The 205-minute schedule

Only the second schedule keeps its peak at or below three. The extra ten minutes show the completion-time cost of choosing between these two plans.

A planning model lists several individually feasible berth-and-crane plans for each ship. What must it do before publishing a schedule for the whole quay?
Answer and reasoning

Choose one option per ship and check the combined use of quay space and cranes over time

Vessel-level options are building blocks. Two individually feasible options can occupy the same berth or claim the same crane at the same time.

A crane operator reports that a box is in a different stack from the one in the scheduling system. What should the planner do before assigning its pickup?
Answer and reasoning

Verify the box’s actual slot and check the revised move against the stack and crane constraints

The actual stack determines whether the crane can reach the box and which moves must precede retrieval. A stale position can make the scheduled move physically impossible.

A proposed yard layout sends internal vehicles and outside trucks to opposite ends of a storage block. Which comparison would best explain its effect?
Answer and reasoning

Trace each vehicle route, count shared crossings and compare travel distance

Separating entrances can reduce conflicts while changing how far vehicles travel. Following both routes makes each effect visible.

Two ships are now expected together after one was delayed. A manager proposes keeping a crane in reserve on similar days. What evidence would help decide whether that policy is worthwhile?
Answer and reasoning

The cost of holding the crane available and the vessel delay it would avoid across plausible arrivals

A reserve has an opportunity cost. Comparing that cost with the waiting it can prevent gives the terminal a basis for deciding how much slack to keep.
