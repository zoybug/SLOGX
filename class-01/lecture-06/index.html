Lecture 06 / 27 October 2026

Introduction to Instant Delivery with Drones

The lecture follows a Meituan order through the merchant, rider, dispatch station, flight and collection point. Ground handling, supervision, weather and customer access all affect whether the service saves time.

LecturerProf. Xinlei Chen

Xinlei Chen is an associate professor at Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School. His research includes intelligent Internet of Things, multi-agent collaboration, pervasive computing, robotics and drone swarms. The supplied lecture introduces his work on low-latency response and collaborative aerial and ground sensing.

The assignment

Student work

Write two pages giving your view of drone delivery. The brief suggested benefits and shortcomings, the delivery process, urban technical challenges, current problems and possible improvements. Students could choose other angles.

How the work was read

The synthesis covers 32 reports. The analysis distinguishes field observations, company examples and student proposals. The summaries omit names, identifiers and grades.

Read the 2025 class on instant delivery by drone

Start with the road trip the flight replaces

Students saw a stronger case for a small urgent parcel crossing water than for a routine package already on a well-loaded van.

Read the analysis

Reports repeatedly asked what the customer would otherwise have to do. A light shipment to a clinic or a location across water can face a long road detour or a wait for a ferry. In those examples, flying one package could buy time that matters to the recipient. Students were more divided about ordinary meals and routine parcels. Some valued the chance to bypass traffic and reduce pressure on riders. Others pointed out that a van can share a journey among many deliveries. The comparison depends on urgency, payload and the actual ground alternative. The reports offer candidate routes for a pilot, rather than measurements of savings on those routes.

Follow the order beyond touchdown

The class’s delivery diagrams kept going after landing. Someone still has to receive the parcel, and the customer may have a walk to the locker.

Read the analysis

Students reconstructed the station-based service as a relay. A merchant prepares the order, a rider takes it to a departure point, staff or equipment load it, and the customer collects it at the receiving station. Some reports saw an advantage for riders who could stop at the hub. Others followed the time from the customer’s perspective and asked how a distant locker, a queue or a delayed notification would affect the result. A fast flight can fit inside a slow order. A fair trial would time the same start and finish for both services, such as order acceptance and collection by the customer.

Plan for the day the aircraft stays on the ground

Wind, rain and an unavailable landing point turned a routing question into a customer-service question. Who takes the order next?

Read the analysis

The reports treated weather as an operating condition with consequences throughout the delivery. A route that looked suitable at dispatch might become unusable while orders were being prepared. A full receiving station or a sudden change in wind could also interrupt the planned handoff. Students proposed checking route and station conditions before accepting an order, informing the customer of delays, and keeping a ground courier available when service was suspended. Several wanted a gradual rollout on predictable corridors. This changes how to evaluate a pilot. Count cancelled orders and time spent waiting for an alternative alongside the deliveries completed in good conditions.

Count the facilities and the neighbours

A fleet needs places to load, charge, maintain and collect. People living beside those sites experience the service even if they never order from it.

Read the analysis

Students connected infrastructure costs with decisions about who would use a station. Shared collection sites and lockers could spread the cost over more orders, while a site far from customers could erase the convenience that attracted them. Other reports asked who would notice repeated overflights, how operators should handle complaints, and what residents could learn about cameras or incident procedures. Environmental arguments also depended on the comparison. An electric flight replacing a long road detour differs from an extra trip added to a van route that still runs. These are questions for a site trial involving customers, staff and nearby residents.

When does the flight save time?

A rider can reach the customer in 22 minutes. The station service takes 5 minutes for ground transfer and 6 for flight. Add the dispatch queue and the customer’s collection time.

With 4 minutes for dispatch and 9 for collection, station delivery takes 24 minutes. The rider is 2 minutes faster.

Self-check

Decision quiz

Twelve short scenarios. Choose a response and check the reasoning behind it.

Which pair of events gives a fair comparison between a drone order and a rider order?
Answer and reasoning

The same order-ready time and the time the customer receives each parcel.

Comparable endpoints include the ground stages and any wait or walk required for collection.

Which proposed pilot has the clearest reason to examine a drone route?
Answer and reasoning

A small urgent shipment to a clinic facing a long road detour.

Payload, urgency and the trip being displaced make this a plausible candidate. Weather, permissions and handoffs would still require evaluation.

The forecast closes a route after several orders have been accepted. Which pilot measure captures the impact on customers?
Answer and reasoning

Time until those customers receive the order through an alternative service.

Fallback completion time captures the disruption affecting real orders, including those excluded from successful-flight averages.

A proposal says an electric drone is greener than the current service. Which data would best support the comparison?
Answer and reasoning

Energy per completed delivery, including ground transfers and the vehicle trip actually displaced.

The comparison depends on the whole service and what changes in the ground network. A van that still runs may save little energy.

A station serves more flights after a timetable change, but customers wait longer for orders. Which pair of records would help locate the bottleneck?
Answer and reasoning

The arrival and release times at loading and pickup, paired by order.

Time stamps at each handoff show whether a queue formed before the flight or after the parcel arrived.

A customer’s order reaches a delivery locker, but the customer never receives a pickup notification. Which status should the operator record?
Answer and reasoning

Arrived at the collection point, with customer handover still outstanding.

Station arrival and customer collection are separate events. Recording them separately reveals a failure at the final handoff.

Two candidate pickup sites have similar rent. One is farther from housing and difficult to reach on foot. Which evidence would best inform the site decision?
Answer and reasoning

Customer walking times and accessibility alongside expected demand at each site.

The collection leg affects convenience and who can use the service. Comparable rents do not imply comparable access.

A resident raises concerns about cameras near a proposed pickup station. Which response directly addresses the issue?
Answer and reasoning

Explain what data are collected, who can access them and how complaints will be handled.

The concern is about privacy and accountability. An operating policy and a practical way to raise concerns address that question.

Two companies propose sharing a collection station to reduce cost. Which question should be resolved before service starts?
Answer and reasoning

How slots, parcel identities and responsibility for a failed handoff will be coordinated.

Shared facilities couple the operators’ schedules and parcel-handling processes. Each handoff needs a clear owner and a way to identify the correct parcel.

A service can dispatch ten orders in a period, but its only receiving station can clear six parcels during that period. What should the booking system consider?
Answer and reasoning

Receiving capacity and existing reservations before promising pickup times.

A receiving station can limit throughput even when aircraft and packages qualify for dispatch.

A service wants to assess disturbance near a residential station. Which pilot data would be most informative?
Answer and reasoning

Noise observations and resident reports by location, time and frequency of service.

Residents experience particular overflights at particular times. Locally matched exposure and complaint records can reveal patterns a fleet average hides.

A finance team compares staffing for a rider service with staffing for a drone service. Which work belongs in the drone-service estimate?
Answer and reasoning

Loading, supervision, maintenance and exception handling as well as the remaining rider work.

A flight changes the mix of tasks. The full labour comparison includes work before takeoff, after landing and when normal operations are interrupted.
